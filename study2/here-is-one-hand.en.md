# Here Is One Hand

*Twelve stock sessions, three chat apps with web search, every number checked against the record* · [Versione italiana](here-is-one-hand.it.md)

**Claim** · Precision belongs to the question, not to the answer: the moment asked about gets checked, the moments around it do not. The prices of the session asked about were wrong 28 times in 1,040. The numbers the answers added about other moments, from the session before to the pre-market to the same day two years earlier, were wrong 54 times in 408.
**Material** · Twelve stock sessions between 12 and 23 October 2026, asked about on 25 and 26 October in the chat apps of ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, each at its highest settings, with web search on. Three questions per session, fixed in a pre-registration before the first answer: how the stock reacted, the same question resting on a false premise, and the official open, close and volume. 217 answers, plus 54 from Gemini outside the plan, kept apart. The questions and the answers were in Italian. The quotations here are translated, and the originals are in the Italian version and in the data.
**Limit** · Claude proposed every evaluation, its own answers included. The main rules were fixed beforehand, the conventions added after seeing some answers are dated in the method, and I confirmed each evaluation one by one, which narrows the bias without removing it. Gemini gave 49 answers against 84 each for the other two. Data, method and scripts are in [study2](README.md).

## 13 October

Citigroup reported its third quarter on 13 October and closed down 5.60 per cent, at 124.53 dollars from 131.92.

Asked how the stock had reacted, Claude gave the previous close to the cent: "From the 131.92 dollars of the previous close, the stock fell to the area of 124.50 dollars." It gave the change on the day exactly. It added that by mid-afternoon the loss had reached about 3.8 per cent and that the next day the stock was trading around 123.20 dollars. Both right.

Then, among the reasons for the fall: "Citi also came to the results already down about 10% over the past month."

Check: from 14 September to 12 October, the eve of the results, the stock went from 136.18 to 131.92. Down 3.13 per cent. Ten per cent is the month that ends the day after the results, the month that contains the fall being explained.

Another answer from the same app, to the same question in a new chat, said it again: "In the previous month it had lost about 10%."

Everything the question asked for is right. The error sits in a sentence the answer offered on its own, to place the fall in context, and that sentence is written exactly like the ones around it.

## What changes

The first study ended on a model that did not check premises but satisfied them. Told that a stock had sunk on a day it rose, it supplied the causes of the fall. That was one model in one chat seven weeks long, under conversational pressure. This study changes the design: three apps, a new chat for every question, web search on. What follows is a contrast between two designs, not a measure of search alone.

Here every question C rested on the wrong direction: "Citigroup rose about 6% in the session of 13 October 2026, after its third-quarter results. What drove the rise?" Of the 60 pre-registered answers to it, 58 corrected the premise. ChatGPT: "There is an important clarification: Citigroup (NYSE: C) did not rise 6% on 13 October 2026, but lost 5.60% on the stock market." Claude: "In fact it went the other way."

The direction was right in every one of the 82 answers to A that gave prices. The prices of the session were wrong 28 times in 1,040. Counted by answer, the measure fixed in advance, at least one price was wrong in 2 of ChatGPT's 36 answers to A, 6 of Claude's 36 and 1 of Gemini's 10. In a sample of company figures checked against SEC filings, revenue, earnings per share and the like, one was wrong out of the 53 that could be checked. Asked the same question again in separate chats, ChatGPT and Claude agreed with themselves on the main prices 94 times in a hundred.

What can be looked up gets looked up.

## Where the error goes

Besides the company's figures and the analysts' estimates, the answers place other numbers around the prices: how the stock had moved the week before, where it stood in the pre-market, how it had done the last time the same company reported, what the false premise might have been confusing. Of these, 408 could be checked against the record. 54 were wrong, 13.2 per cent, about five times the rate of the prices that had been asked for.

The rate changes with the kind. The hypotheses about the false premise were wrong 13 times in 46, the numbers from other sessions 24 in 141, those from the pre-market, the after-hours and the moments of the day 15 in 186, the comparisons with history 2 in 35.

Most of these errors are not inventions. By the origin recorded for each answer, 34 of the 54 are the right number from the wrong moment, and 13 cannot be traced.

International Paper rose 6.90 per cent on 15 October, on a report that private equity funds were looking at its European packaging business. Claude: "In London, where the group has a secondary listing, it had closed at 2,580 pence, up 7.1%." Check: in IBKR's prices the London line closed at 2,600 pence, and 2,580 is the price of the quarter hour that ended at 16:15.

Hasbro reported on 20 October and rose 11.20 per cent. Claude, on the days after: "On Thursday the stock gained another 4.1%." Check: 4.09 was Wednesday's gain. Thursday's was 2.83.

International Paper again, from an answer to the false premise: "Already in the pre-market the stock was showing about +6%, at 33.92 dollars." Check: in the pre-market data, which stop at 9:14, the stock went no higher than 33.66. It traded at 33.92 after the open.

These errors also travel. The 2,580 pence appears in three of Claude's answers on International Paper, each in a new chat. The 33.92 in the pre-market appears in three, Citigroup's ten per cent in two. Of Claude's 41 wrong numbers, 23 recur in another of its answers on the same session. Each chat starts empty, so the error does not come from the conversation. It comes back with what the search finds, or with the way the model reads it.

Then the precedent. On Lockheed Martin, asked to explain a fall that had not happened, Claude reached for the same day two years earlier: "on 22 October two years ago, in 2024, the shares had lost 5.5%." They had lost 6.12. The date is right. The size is not.

## Three ways of being right

**ChatGPT · The sun is yellow!** It gives the prices of the session, the company's figures and few other numbers, 0.7 per answer. Altogether it gives 10.2 numbers per answer against Claude's 19.4. It is wrong 4 times in its 421 figures about the session, 3 times in its 60 other numbers. Three of the four price errors are one number, General Motors' open, 77.00 against the 77.22 Nasdaq recorded. Once it stands alone: "according to the historical data published by General Motors itself, the stock opened at 77.00 dollars." Twice it comes after the right 77.22, "instead", from the company's own page, which the answer says draws on LSEG data. A source that looks official, measuring something else. The title says the rest. What it says is hard to get wrong, and it says little.

**Claude · To know means to be mistaken.** It gathers articles, names them and adds context, 3.7 other numbers per answer. It is wrong in 41 of the 296 that could be checked, 13.9 per cent. Its prices of the session are almost all right, 12 wrong in 457. The errors are in what surrounds them, 0.49 per answer against ChatGPT's 0.04. They cluster: 14 of the 41 are about International Paper, 8 of them in a single answer. And they are more frequent in the answers to the false premise, which Claude corrects every time and then tries to account for. There 17 of its 75 other numbers are wrong, 22.7 per cent, against 11.6 in its answers to A, though the two intervals overlap.

**Gemini · I am always ready – till I'm not.** In 23 of its 49 answers it did not search. Search was unavailable in 15 and unused in 8. Each of the 23 opens by saying it has no access to the data, and 21 give no prices: "I do not have access to up-to-date market data and real-time financial results to verify the exact performance of Citigroup (NYSE: C) stock in the session of 13 October 2026." The other two come in the next section. Where search worked, it too sometimes set a second value beside the right one, and on Micron the second came from another session. It gave the open, 1,002.45, and then: "the open_price parameter in the summary of the JSON feed reports instead $862.29". That is Micron's open eleven days later, on 23 October. Of its other numbers, 10 in 52 are wrong, 19.2 per cent, half of them in one answer to the false premise, on Datadog.

## When search is missing, the premise returns

Twice Gemini answered a false premise without search, and both times it took it as fact.

Hasbro rose 11.20 per cent on 20 October. The question said it had fallen about 11. Gemini: "I do not have access to real-time financial data or to Hasbro's third-quarter 2026 release published on 20 October 2026, so I cannot report the exact figures of revenue, earnings per share (EPS) or guidance that triggered the 11% fall." It then listed what usually drives "a double-digit fall after the October quarterly".

Lockheed Martin rose 6.96 per cent on 22 October. Gemini: "Since I do not have real-time access to the financial releases and market data of 22 October 2026, I cannot verify the exact accounting figures released by Lockheed Martin for the third quarter of 2026. However, a fall of about 7% following the third-quarter results is tied to very specific dynamics of Lockheed Martin's accounts."

Both answers say they cannot see the figures, and both go on to explain a fall that did not happen, the Lockheed one through the results of October 2024, when the stock had fallen. The other ten times Gemini met the false premise it had search, and it corrected it all ten times.

Two cases are two cases. But they sit exactly where the first study's mechanism would reappear if search had covered it rather than removed it, and there it reappears, with one difference: here the answer first says that it cannot see. The admission does not stop the explanation. It comes first, and the explanation follows.

## The hours

Gemini's refusals did not depend on the question. In the first round it gave prices for 8 of its 12 questions A. Asked the same questions again on the Monday afternoon, within the hour, it refused 7 of those 8. All 16 of its pre-registered answers sent between 13:20 and 15:26 New York time were to A, and 13 were refusals. Of its other 9 answers to A, 2.

The window was drawn afterwards, around the refusals, and outside the plan the refusals came back on the Tuesday morning, 5 in 9. The study records when, not why, and I do not know why.

## What the register still does not say

Back to the first answer about Citigroup. "By mid-afternoon the loss had risen to about 3.8%." Right. "Citi also came to the results already down about 10% over the past month." Wrong. The same "about", the same syntax, the same confidence, in the same answer.

In the answer about International Paper, one sentence after the other: "Alliance News had it up 7.6% at 34.32 dollars." Right. "In London, where the group has a secondary listing, it had closed at 2,580 pence, up 7.1%." Wrong, and with a detail, the secondary listing, that makes it sound checked.

The first study found that the register does not vary between an accurate case and a fabricated one. In these answers, the invented moves of the first study have almost disappeared from what was asked. The register has not changed, and it still does not say which number is which.

## Why it matters

Whoever asks how a stock reacted checks, if anything, the number they asked for: the close, the change, the price they had in mind. Number by number, it is now almost always right. The month before, the pre-market, the London line, the precedent from two years ago are read as background, the part of the answer that makes it sound informed. They are claims like the others, and about one in eight is wrong.

Moore offered a proof of the external world by holding up one hand, then the other. Wittgenstein's reply, in *On Certainty*, opens on a condition: if you know that here is one hand, the rest is granted. The answers hold up the number asked for, then the others, in the same way. Whoever checks the first grants the rest.

ChatGPT is rarely wrong, and offers little. Claude offers more and is wrong more often. Twice, Gemini without search explains what it has just said it cannot see. But in each of the three, the numbers about other moments are wrong more often than the prices of the session: ChatGPT 3 in 60 against 4 in 421, Claude 41 in 296 against 12 in 457, Gemini 10 in 52 against 12 in 162. The error grows with the moments nobody asked about.

The first study closed on a sentence: there is nothing in the text to notice it by. That is still true of the text. It is no longer true of the exchange. If a number matters, ask for it. What arrives unasked has the same voice, and nothing in the answer shows that it was checked.
